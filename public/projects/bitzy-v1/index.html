01 / Design intent

A robot built to be developed.

Bitzy is the physical robot developed under KineArm. An earlier six-axis kinematics study evolved into a built five-axis arm with printed mechanics, paired shoulder actuation and a common star/four-screw tool interface.

The project connects mechanical design, fabrication, calibration, embedded control and editable teach-and-replay motion. Gripper and screwdriver tooling share the original mounting interface so their mechanisms can develop independently.

Read the case study

Built hardware. Continuing engineering.

The prototype is moving. Gripper Rev F is printed, fitted and driven from the control page, and screwdriver R2 has completed CAD and print packages. Tool force, load and durability, positioning accuracy and repeatability have not been established quantitatively.

07 / Local AI decisions

A 27B model deciding on one RTX 4090.

Experimental decision software on the built prototype, September 2026. Bitzy takes a plain-English instruction and a camera view, and a vision-language model running locally proposes one bounded action. Recorded software evidence only; no positioning, payload or accuracy claim.

  • Local modelQwen3.8-27B, 4-bit, with 55 of 64 blocks on one RTX 4090 and 9 on the CPU. No cloud calls. A decision with an image currently takes several minutes.
  • Bounded outputThe model answers with one JSON intent from a short allowlist: pick a listed object, move the tool, go to a marker, answer a question or decline. It never outputs coordinates or servo angles.
  • Checked before motionBitzy rejects malformed or unmeasured choices and plans made stale by STOP, a camera or arm change, or a missing target. Motion needs operator permission and runs through the existing guarded tasks; STOP never waits for the model.
  • One coordinate frameMarkers, detected objects, the tool tip and move steps share the frame printed on the table sheet, so the model's instructions and Bitzy's measurements refer to the same axes.
  • Visible activityThe control page draws sampled forward-hook values for Bitzy's own decision and freezes when inference ends. In one recorded three-step run, all 802,784 displayed values matched the model's saved activations. The vision encoder is not instrumented.
  • Real-arm runsIn supervised runs on the real arm, the model picked out the listed GoPro mount as its target and, when asked to bring it toward marker 3, chose steps toward marker 3; Bitzy ran those choices through its guarded pick and move tasks. One answer was rejected for an extra field, one plan was discarded as stale, and one decision stopped with STOP had its late answer recorded but not used.

I label move steps by hand as Correct, Wrong or Unsafe, stored apart from the model's own output; so far some are Correct and some Wrong, and Wrong or Unsafe steps never become training examples. Our current reading is that the camera-to-arm calibration, more than the model's choice, limits the physical result.

Training data

Simulator examples

3,000 randomised simulator scenes with rule-based labels, each accepted by Bitzy's validator, plus filtered real decisions and my labels.

  • Why a smaller modelSeveral minutes per decision is too slow for a responsive arm, so the 27B's role is being distilled into Qwen3.5-2B with LoRA adapters on its language layers.
  • Overnight training run3,000 optimizer steps in about 121 minutes on the RTX 4090 with the 27B paused: about 4.4 passes over 2,737 training examples (2,730 simulated, 7 from real decisions), with 15.6 M trainable adapter parameters.
  • Held-out checkOn 270 held-out simulator scenes, every answer was valid and 266 matched the rule answer exactly, at 1.6 s median per answer; most misses named the wrong one of two similar parts. These scenes resemble its training data, so this is not a real-world result. Only 2 real decisions were held out and 1 matched, too few to judge.
  • Second training runA retrain on the next simulator version matched the rule answer on 265 of 281 held-out simulator scenes and on both of 2 held-out real decisions, still too few real examples to judge. This is the adapter now running in shadow mode.
  • Shadow modeThe student now runs in shadow mode on the CPU, so it never competes with the 27B for GPU memory. It answers each decision after the 27B, with its own live activity view, and its answers are recorded but never executed. Its first shadow answer was valid but repeated an answer memorised from training instead of describing the image.
  • One 24 GB GPUTraining beside the loaded 27B pushed it out of GPU memory, so training now refuses to start while the 27B is loaded and runs on a schedule that pauses and restarts it.
08 / Gripper module

A servo gripper on the same wrist.

Gripper Rev F, printed, assembled and fitted to the built prototype in September 2026. It mounts on the same interface as the magnet module used for the AI picks and runs from the KineArm control page. Recorded build and software evidence only; grip force, payload and positioning are not measured.

  • Printed and fittedAn MG90S micro servo turns a pinion that moves both jaws together. On the printed part the body stands 69.8 mm above the module seat and the jaws add 31.831 mm.
  • Its own tool lengthThe control page now has a tool-module menu. Choosing the gripper moves the inverse-kinematics tool point to the fingertips, 141.631 mm past the J5 axis instead of 75 mm for the magnet module, and limits the tool channel to the gripper's travel. Modules switch only while the arm is disarmed, and the arm cannot be armed until the saved module has loaded.
  • Open, close and calibrateThe MG90S runs on channel 6 of the PCA9685 servo driver. OPEN and CLOSE buttons drive the jaws, and each end point can be set from the current position and saved with the controller settings.
  • 50 Hz servo signalThe firmware drove every servo channel at 200 Hz, but the MG90S is only documented at 50 Hz. The ESP32-S3 firmware now runs at 50 Hz; it was reflashed and the setting read back from the driver board.
  • Markers for the cameraPrinted ArUco strips with two markers each were made for both jaws and both wide faces of the body, using IDs separate from the magnet module's, and every ID was checked by decoding the printed sheet. Teaching Bitzy Vision to switch marker sets and tool geometry with the module is the next step.
  • Bugs found in first useAfter OPEN or CLOSE, the next pitch or scroll-wheel input could swing the wrist, because the page re-derived its aim from a pose where only the tool had moved. That was fixed, and a review of the controller, Bitzy Vision and its training tools fixed 27 further issues, such as HOME and replays resetting the gripper, with tests for the fixes.
